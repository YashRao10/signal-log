New here? Quick glossary of terms used on this site
Thesis
The specific reason behind a trade — what has to be true for it to work out, not just "I think it'll go up."
Buy / Sell / Trim
Buy = starting or adding to a position. Sell = exiting it fully. Trim = selling only part of it, usually to lock in gains while keeping the rest.
Starter / Building / Core
How much conviction a position carries relative to everything else — a new idea starts small (Starter), grows as the thesis proves out (Building), and becomes a long-term holding once it has (Core). No dollar amounts, just relative sizing.
Sector ETF
A single fund that holds many companies in one industry (e.g. XLV = healthcare, XLE = energy) — a way to invest in a whole sector at once instead of picking individual stocks.
Yield curve / "2s10s"
The gap between short-term and long-term government bond interest rates. When short-term rates rise faster than long-term ones (the curve "flattens"), it usually means investors expect less near-term Fed rate-cutting than before.
DCA (dollar-cost averaging)
Buying a fixed amount on a regular schedule (weekly, biweekly) instead of all at once — smooths out the effect of short-term price swings.
CPI / Inflation
A measure of how much prices for everyday goods are rising. Higher CPI growth = inflation running hotter, which affects Fed rate decisions.
Earnings
The quarterly report where a public company reveals actual revenue/profit — often the single biggest price-moving event for a stock.
"Since entry / since sold"
How much the stock's price has moved from the date of the trade to today — pure public price data, not tied to how much money was actually invested.

A Weekend Gut-Check: Which Drawdowns Are Real, and Which Are Just Cost-Basis Math
Jul 11, 2026 · Market Analysis

Every laggard in a portfolio looks the same on a screen — a red percentage next to a ticker. They are not the same underneath, and a weekend is a good time to actually check rather than assume. Five names that have been underwater for a while got the same question this week: is the decline a broken thesis, or just an unlucky entry point relative to where the market has since moved?

Gold's ETF proxy is down meaningfully from where it was bought — but that entry came within days of an all-time-high spike in late January, right before a sharp one-day crash tied to a Fed-chair nomination headline. Spot gold has been grinding back since, sitting near multi-week highs this week as the Iran/Strait-of-Hormuz tension flared again, and both HSBC's and JP Morgan's medium-term targets still imply real upside from current levels. That's a "bad entry timing, not a bad thesis" story.

The two energy majors and the defense name in this bucket tell a similar story from a different angle: all three were bought during a mid-2026 trough, and all three are now catching a real tailwind — energy from the Hormuz-driven oil spike, defense from a fresh multi-billion-dollar acquisition and a NATO allied-spending commitment. Both were actually green on the most recent session, even while sitting underwater on a cost-basis view. A mega-cap tech name in the same bucket is down nearly 30% from its own 52-week high despite genuinely strong underlying numbers (cloud growth in the 40% range, a multi-billion AI revenue run-rate) — the market's real argument there is about the timing of AI-capex payoff, not whether the business is winning, and consensus price targets still imply substantial upside.

The one name that came out of this review looking different: a newly-public space-launch company down over 20% from its post-IPO entry. Unlike the others, this one has real, specific news behind the move — a large bond offering that reignited capital-intensity concerns, a valuation still around 100x sales even after the pullback, and a slow-motion float expansion from upcoming share-lockup expirations. That's not "the market being emotional about a fine business" — it's a genuine, still-unresolved risk that deserves an actual watch level rather than being waved off as noise.

The takeaway: "down a lot" and "thesis broken" are different claims, and conflating them either way is a mistake — holding something out of inertia because "it'll come back" is just as sloppy as panic-selling something that's actually fine. The discipline is checking each one on its own terms, on a schedule, not just when it's already scary.

High-multiple software names are the most exposed part of the market to the hawkish rate repricing covered in yesterday's note on this log — when the market prices in more rate-cut delay (or a hike), the present value of a distant growth story shrinks mechanically, independent of anything the company itself did today.

The practical read: "AI stocks were mixed today" is a true but low-information sentence. A demand-story re-rating (Meta), a credibility/fraud-adjacent allegation (Bloom Energy), and a rate-driven multiple compression (Palo Alto Networks) are three different risks that happen to be showing up in adjacent tickers on the same day. Worth tracking each on its own terms rather than reading them as one signal about "the AI trade."

The Fed's Hawkish Pivot — Why "No Rate Cut" Wasn't the Real Story
Jul 10, 2026 · Market Analysis

The Fed's June 17 meeting held the funds rate steady at 3.50-3.75% — unsurprising, and mostly priced in already. The real news was buried in the dot plot: under new Chair Kevin Warsh, 9 of 18 committee members now project at least one hike by year-end, a reversal from the cut-leaning projections markets had been pricing through most of H1. Futures markets have since started pricing a real possibility of a hike as early as October.

This matters more than a single data point because it changes the direction of the discount-rate story that's been the backdrop for every rate-sensitive name on this log. A market pricing eventual cuts and a market pricing a possible hike arrive at very different valuations for the same long-duration growth cash flows — the 2s10s dynamic covered here back in early July was already flagging the curve moving in an unfriendly direction; this is the policy-side confirmation of why.

The practical read: sectors with pricing power, steadier demand, or a direct AI-infrastructure angle that isn't pure mega-cap software (utilities, energy, industrials) have had a stronger run in 2026's rotation than the names most exposed to a "cuts are still coming" thesis. That's not a reason to abandon the AI-buildout story — the demand signal hasn't changed — but it is a reason to be more selective about paying up for further length in names that already re-rated sharply higher since June, rather than adding indiscriminately across the whole growth complex.

What to watch: whether the "at least one hike" contingent on the committee grows or shrinks heading into the next meeting, and whether the October hike odds currently being priced actually firm up or fade as new data arrives. Either direction is a bigger swing factor for this kind of book right now than any single earnings print.

Zooming out past the US: the pan-European STOXX 600 gained just under 2% on the week, while Japan was mixed — the Nikkei 225 slipped slightly, but the broader TOPIX index gained. Markets are closed Friday July 3rd (observed) and Saturday July 4th itself, reopening Monday July 6th. The new Fed Chair has also moved toward a more purely data-dependent approach and away from traditional forward guidance, which likely means more of these single-data-point swings rather than fewer.

AI's Power Bottleneck: Why Compute Isn't the Constraint Anymore
Jun 2026 · Market Analysis

The AI buildout narrative has quietly shifted. Through 2023–2025 the gating constraint was compute — GPU supply, fab capacity, chip yields. Heading into H2 2026, the constraint is increasingly electricity: data center power demand is projected to keep climbing sharply through 2030, and US data center capacity is set to nearly triple over the same window. Nvidia itself has started explicitly linking future AI growth projections to power availability, not chip supply.

That reframing matters for how the AI trade gets played. It's no longer just semiconductors and hyperscalers — it's the power producers and grid-adjacent names sitting behind them. Unregulated power producers with direct long-term supply agreements to hyperscalers are a cleaner, more contracted way to get AI-adjacent exposure than chasing valuation on the chip names directly. Same logic applies to grid buildout, cooling infrastructure, and anything that turns electrons into usable data-center capacity.

The risk to watch: this is now a crowded, well-understood thesis. The "power is the bottleneck" trade has been running for a while, and the names most levered to it can move sharply on single-day news — a lost contract, a competing technology announcement, insider selling — even when the multi-year thesis hasn't changed at all.

The 2s10s Curve and What It's Telling Us
Jul 2, 2026 · Market Analysis

The 2-Year Treasury yield has climbed faster than the 10-Year through H1 2026 — a flattening move in the yield curve. That's a market signal about the *path* of rates, not the level: the 2-year reflects where traders expect the Fed to be over the next couple of years, while the effective Fed funds rate itself has barely moved. When the 2-year rises while the policy rate sits still, it means the market is pricing in less near-term easing than it previously expected — not that policy has tightened.

See the chart in the Econ Briefing tab for the actual data. The practical read for equities: long-duration, high-multiple growth names are the most exposed to this kind of repricing, since their valuations lean more heavily on cash flows far in the future getting discounted at whatever rate the market settles on. It's a slower, quieter risk than a Fed rate hike — but it shows up in exactly the kind of pullback tech saw earlier this year before the H1 rally resumed.

How I Think About Risk
Jul 2, 2026 · Methodology

A few ground rules that shape every move on this log, independent of any single ticker's story:

Conviction sets position size, not price. A brand-new idea starts as a small starter position — enough to track it seriously, not enough to hurt if the thesis is wrong. It only grows into a core holding after the thesis has actually played out for a while, not because the stock went up fast.

A thesis break is a sell signal; a price drop alone isn't. The BE trim and the PANW/AMD/GLD profit-taking in the log are both examples: sizing changed because something about the setup changed (a lost contract, a valuation that ran ahead of itself), not because the position was simply down or simply up. Noise gets held through; a broken thesis gets acted on.

Diversify across what actually drives returns, not just ticker count. Ten AI-adjacent tickers isn't diversification if they all move on the same headline. The sector-gap fills on this log (Healthcare, Financials, Energy, Defense, Utilities) exist specifically to reduce correlation to the AI trade, not to check a box.

Earnings are a hold decision, not a trade decision, unless something's actually broken. Positions built on a multi-quarter thesis get held through the print that thesis depends on — MSFT and META both get held through their July earnings for exactly this reason. Exiting right before the event that's supposed to prove the thesis defeats the point of having one.
