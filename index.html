New here? Quick glossary of terms used on this site
Thesis
The specific reason behind a trade — what has to be true for it to work out, not just "I think it'll go up."
Buy / Sell / Trim
Buy = starting or adding to a position. Sell = exiting it fully. Trim = selling only part of it, usually to lock in gains while keeping the rest.
Starter / Building / Core
How much conviction a position carries relative to everything else — a new idea starts small (Starter), grows as the thesis proves out (Building), and becomes a long-term holding once it has (Core). No dollar amounts, just relative sizing.
Sector ETF
A single fund that holds many companies in one industry (e.g. XLV = healthcare, XLE = energy) — a way to invest in a whole sector at once instead of picking individual stocks.
Yield curve / "2s10s"
The gap between short-term and long-term government bond interest rates. When short-term rates rise faster than long-term ones (the curve "flattens"), it usually means investors expect less near-term Fed rate-cutting than before.
DCA (dollar-cost averaging)
Buying a fixed amount on a regular schedule (weekly, biweekly) instead of all at once — smooths out the effect of short-term price swings.
CPI / Inflation
A measure of how much prices for everyday goods are rising. Higher CPI growth = inflation running hotter, which affects Fed rate decisions.
Earnings
The quarterly report where a public company reveals actual revenue/profit — often the single biggest price-moving event for a stock.
"Since entry / since sold"
How much the stock's price has moved from the date of the trade to today — pure public price data, not tied to how much money was actually invested.

The same evening, Bloom Energy's Q2 print resolved the trim-vs-hold question flagged on this log a few days earlier — a genuine beat, not the feared miss, with no new post-earnings low.

Two names with a similar AI-capex story, one clean beat and one real miss, on the same earnings night.

What to watch: Apple and Amazon both report tonight (Jul 30, after close) — the last pair of the busiest earnings stretch this log has tracked all summer. A China Lithography Breakthrough Crashes Chip Stocks While Oil Crashes for a Different Reason Entirely
Jul 27, 2026 · Market Analysis

Semiconductor stocks sold off sharply Monday afternoon — figures below are intraday, as of roughly 2:40pm ET, with the market still open — after a report that a Shanghai state-backed firm has begun mass-producing homegrown immersion DUV lithography machines, the class of equipment used to manufacture advanced chips. The news raised fresh fears about how quickly China can build domestic chipmaking capacity independent of Western suppliers like ASML. AMD led the decliners, down 7.5% intraday, with Teradyne down 6.1%, Nvidia down 5.1%, the semiconductor-sector ETF SMH down 3.7%, and Micron down 4.5%. Micron's drop carried a second catalyst on top of the equipment headline: Chinese memory maker ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) went public in Shanghai and saw its shares soar more than 500% on debut, instantly making it the world's fourth-largest DRAM maker at roughly 8% global market share, with reports that Apple has begun testing its chips — reviving the same China-competition anxiety on the memory side. The selling also picked up spillover momentum from a negative post-earnings reaction in Intel shares.

CNBC's Jim Cramer, watching the reversal unfold, described the sellers driving it as "monstrous, motivated and often margined" — a reference to leveraged positions unwinding in names like AMD and Intel that had been up sharply for the year. Selling of that kind tends to be harder to arrest than an ordinary pullback driven by buyers simply stepping back, because forced or margin-driven selling doesn't wait for a better price.

Crude oil moved in the opposite direction, falling hard after the US and Iran reportedly paused strikes against each other over the weekend, unwinding much of the war-risk premium that had built into prices on fears of a Strait of Hormuz supply disruption. Brent crude fell about 5.2% to $91.73 a barrel and WTI fell about 5.4% to $84.45, per a Monday-morning read from Rigzone; other outlets had the intraday move running anywhere from roughly 4% to more than 8%, so the decline is best read as landing somewhere in the 5%-to-6% range rather than as a single precise number.

With chip names selling off and oil falling, money rotated into defense, megacap tech, and defensive consumer names. RTX rose 3.4% on a new multi-billion-dollar Department of Defense contract for next-generation integrated air and missile defense systems, adding to a recent beat-and-raise quarter and record backlog. Microsoft gained 2.9% heading into Wednesday's fiscal Q4 report, with Guggenheim reiterating a Buy rating and raising its price target to $586 the same day. Coca-Cola rose 2.2% in a classic rotation into defensive, non-cyclical names ahead of its own Tuesday earnings. Alphabet added 1.9% on broad-based megacap tech buying tied to continued investor enthusiasm for its AI trajectory, including Gemini usage growth. Separately, Palantir jumped 6.7% after Citi analyst Tyler Radke maintained his Buy rating — trimming his price target to $200 from $225 on a lower software-sector valuation multiple, but raising his 2027 revenue-growth forecast to 53%, well above the Street's 45%, and calling for a commercial rebound ahead of the company's own earnings.

The practical read: the net effect on the broad market was muted relative to the size of the individual moves. The S&P 500 slipped roughly a quarter to a third of a percent to 7391.17, the Nasdaq-100 fell about 0.9% to 27873.28, the small-cap Russell 2000 actually held slightly positive at 2937.92, and the VIX rose about 4.7% to 19.46 — a sign of genuine risk-off tone underneath the surface even with oil falling. Today isn't one story, it's three running at once: a China-driven derating of chip-equipment and memory names, a geopolitical de-escalation trade in oil, and a rotation into names with their own earnings- or contract-driven catalysts. None of them cancel each other out; they just happened to land on the same tape.

Semiconductors Confirm a Bear Market — And Wall Street Is Already Calling the Bottom
Jul 20, 2026 · Market Analysis

The semiconductor sector has now formally entered a bear market. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) fell roughly 20% from its late-June record, dropping from a peak near 14,655 to about 11,674 by Jul 17. SMH, a broad chip-sector ETF, separately posted its worst single week since March 2025 (down about 11% in that one week) on the way down. Sector ETFs are stabilizing today, with SOXX up 0.41% and SMH up 0.29%, but that's a pause, not a reversal — the underlying index is still deep in bear-market territory.

The proximate cause isn't a rate shock or an earnings miss at any single company — it's a valuation and growth-expectations question. Investors are increasingly skeptical that the current pace of AI infrastructure spending across the industry can keep justifying itself, and that skepticism sharpened after a competitive new Chinese AI model, Kimi K3, chipped away at assumptions about Nvidia's dominance in AI compute. It's a confidence crack in the AI-buildout trade itself, not a breakdown in any one company's fundamentals.

Several sell-side banks are already pushing back with a contrarian, buy-the-dip case. Morgan Stanley named NVDA and AVGO its top AI-compute picks amid the selloff, calling the pullback an attractive entry point. JPMorgan told clients the drawdown is a buying opportunity, citing tight AI chip supply-demand dynamics that it expects to persist through 2028. Mizuho's Vijay Rakesh went further, saying there's "still a lot of gas left in the tank." The market seems to be listening, at least today: NVDA is up 0.25% and AMD is up 1.56%.

The practical read: bulls and bears aren't actually disagreeing about whether AI infrastructure spending continues — both sides assume it does. The disagreement is about whether the returns on that spending will show up fast enough to justify current multiples. That's not a question today's price action answers either way; it's a question the next few quarters of hyperscaler capex guidance and AI-revenue disclosures will have to settle.

AI's Power Bottleneck: Why Compute Isn't the Constraint Anymore
Jun 2026 · Market Analysis

The AI buildout narrative has quietly shifted. Through 2023–2025 the gating constraint was compute — GPU supply, fab capacity, chip yields. Heading into H2 2026, the constraint is increasingly electricity: data center power demand is projected to keep climbing sharply through 2030, and US data center capacity is set to nearly triple over the same window. Nvidia itself has started explicitly linking future AI growth projections to power availability, not chip supply.

That reframing matters for how the AI trade gets played. It's no longer just semiconductors and hyperscalers — it's the power producers and grid-adjacent names sitting behind them. Unregulated power producers with direct long-term supply agreements to hyperscalers are a cleaner, more contracted way to get AI-adjacent exposure than chasing valuation on the chip names directly. Same logic applies to grid buildout, cooling infrastructure, and anything that turns electrons into usable data-center capacity.

The risk to watch: this is now a crowded, well-understood thesis. The "power is the bottleneck" trade has been running for a while, and the names most levered to it can move sharply on single-day news — a lost contract, a competing technology announcement, insider selling — even when the multi-year thesis hasn't changed at all.

The 2s10s Curve and What It's Telling Us
Jul 2, 2026 · Market Analysis

The 2-Year Treasury yield has climbed faster than the 10-Year through H1 2026 — a flattening move in the yield curve. That's a market signal about the *path* of rates, not the level: the 2-year reflects where traders expect the Fed to be over the next couple of years, while the effective Fed funds rate itself has barely moved. When the 2-year rises while the policy rate sits still, it means the market is pricing in less near-term easing than it previously expected — not that policy has tightened.

It's a slower, quieter risk than a Fed rate hike — but it shows up in exactly the kind of pullback tech saw earlier this year before the H1 rally resumed.

How I Think About Risk
Jul 2, 2026 · Methodology

A few ground rules that shape every move on this log, independent of any single ticker's story:

Conviction sets position size, not price. A brand-new idea starts as a small starter position — enough to track it seriously, not enough to hurt if the thesis is wrong. It only grows into a core holding after the thesis has actually played out for a while, not because the stock went up fast.

A thesis break is a sell signal; a price drop alone isn't. The BE trim and the PANW/AMD/GLD profit-taking in the log are both examples: sizing changed because something about the setup changed (a lost contract, a valuation that ran ahead of itself), not because the position was simply down or simply up. Noise gets held through; a broken thesis gets acted on.

Diversify across what actually drives returns, not just ticker count. Ten AI-adjacent tickers isn't diversification if they all move on the same headline. The sector-gap fills on this log (Healthcare, Financials, Energy, Defense, Utilities) exist specifically to reduce correlation to the AI trade, not to check a box.

Earnings are a hold decision, not a trade decision, unless something's actually broken. Positions built on a multi-quarter thesis get held through the print that thesis depends on — MSFT and META both get held through their July earnings for exactly this reason. Exiting right before the event that's supposed to prove the thesis defeats the point of having one.

The 10-Year Treasury yield has climbed to 4.63% (+0.18pp over the past three months, +0.15pp in just the last month), with the 2-Year moving even faster — up 0.25pp over three months to 4.20%. Fed funds itself hasn't moved (3.63%, flat on the month), so this is the market repricing longer-dated risk on its own, not a policy shift. CPI (~3.7% YoY, June release) and unemployment (4.2%) haven't printed anything new this cycle — no fresh data there to layer on top of the rate move.

Today's session showed the rate mechanism directly: several large growth/tech names sold off with no earnings news attached to any of them — a pure discount-rate story, not a fundamentals one. The more interesting split came from the names that did report: AMD beat Q2 estimates outright on both revenue and EPS, and still fell nearly 6% — the market priced its stepped-up near-term capital spending as a duration risk in a rising-rate backdrop rather than a growth catalyst. Arista Networks, reporting the same evening, also beat and rallied over 6% on a clean beat-and-raise. Same rate environment, same earnings-season beat, opposite reactions — guidance quality is doing more work right now than the headline number.

Gold caught a real bid (+4.4%) opposite the tech weakness, a legitimate flight-to-duration-safety move given the yield backdrop rather than noise.

What to watch: SpaceX (SPCX) just delivered its first-ever public earnings report — a clean beat on revenue and EPS — but the stock has fallen double digits since, as the market weighs an unusually large single-quarter capital-spending number against the growth story. A large share-lockup expiration lands tomorrow (Aug 6), a separate structural catalyst that could add real volatility independent of how the earnings reaction settles. More broadly, if the 10Y/2Y climb keeps extending rather than stabilizing, expect more sessions shaped like today's across rate-sensitive, long-duration names.

FOMC Holds as Expected, Then Microsoft and Meta Give the Market Its Real Earnings Verdict
Jul 30, 2026 · Economic Briefing

Note: no briefing posted Jul 29 — this one covers both sessions using each day's real, official numbers rather than reconstructing intraday color after the fact.

The FOMC held rates at 3.5–3.75% on Jul 29, exactly as consensus expected — the fifth straight hold. The more interesting detail was underneath the headline: three committee members dissented in favor of a 25bp hike, a real and growing minority view that inflation risk (from the Iran-conflict energy premium and AI-infrastructure-driven bottlenecks alike) deserves a tighter response than the majority is giving it. Nothing new to report on the rest of the rate picture — the Fed funds rate sits at 3.63%, the 10-Year Treasury yield at 4.69%, and the 2-Year at 4.33%, all still reflecting the same climb flagged in recent briefings. CPI and unemployment remain on their last prints, both still waiting on their next scheduled release.

Jul 30's real story was earnings, not rates. Microsoft and Meta both reported the night before (Jul 29, after close) with sharply divergent results: Microsoft beat cleanly — Azure grew 43% year-over-year and crossed $100B in full-year revenue for the first time, commercial remaining performance obligations jumped 84% to $678B — while Meta missed on EPS ($6.18 vs. $7.22 expected) and guided revenue light despite still growing revenue 28% year-over-year. The market's reaction made the divergence unmistakable: Microsoft jumped double digits, Meta fell hard, on the same earnings night with a similar AI-capex story behind both.

The index-level numbers confirm this was a broad risk-on session, not a nervous one, even with a real earnings miss in the mix: the S&P 500 rose roughly 0.8% to 7437.63, the Nasdaq-100 outpaced it at roughly +1.9% to 28106.35 on the earnings-driven tech rally, and the VIX — the market's fear gauge — fell sharply, down about 11.7% to 17.09. Broad chip and hardware names (AMD, Dell, Arista, Palo Alto Networks) rallied alongside Microsoft's Azure-capex-confirming beat, underscoring how much weight a single hyperscaler's guidance carries for the rest of the AI-infrastructure trade.

What to watch: Apple and Amazon both report tonight (Jul 30, after close) — the last pair of the busiest earnings stretch this book has tracked all summer. Early-circulating reports suggest Apple beat on both lines but guided weak enough to move shares lower after hours, and Amazon beat on AWS growth — neither is confirmed or reflected in tonight's official closes yet, so treat both as unconfirmed until the numbers actually land.
